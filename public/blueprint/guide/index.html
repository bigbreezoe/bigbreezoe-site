The Beginner Creator Blueprint

The order I wish somebody had handed me. Seven steps, and a prompt at each one you can paste into any AI tool so it does the thinking with you.

This is your copy. Keep the link, it doesn't expire. I don't earn anything from this page and there are no affiliate links in it. The tool pages I point to in step 6 do have some, and they're labeled there. Nothing here promises you'll earn money. It's the order I'm using, written down in case it saves you the year I spent figuring it out.

Read this part first

You probably don't have a motivation problem. You have an order problem.

I spent a long time buying courses, watching videos, and ending most nights with more tabs open than when I started. Nothing made. I thought that meant something was wrong with me.

It didn't. What I was missing was somebody telling me what to do first, what to do second, and what to ignore until later.

That's all this is. Three rules before you start:

  1. Do them in order. Step 4 doesn't work if you skipped step 2.
  2. Don't buy anything until step 6. I mean this.
  3. Finish a step in one sitting. If it's taking longer, you're overthinking it.
STEP 1

Name one person

Not an audience. Not a demographic. One person.

"Beginner creators" is not a person. "Someone with a full-time job who wants to build something on the side and doesn't know what to do first" is a person. You can picture them. You know what they'd say.

This one matters more than the rest, because every decision after it gets easier once it's answered.

Fill in this sentence: I help [who] who struggle with [what] so they can [outcome].

Prompt

I want to start a content channel. Help me find the one specific
person I should be making it for.

Interview me one question at a time. Ask me about:
- what people already come to me for
- what I figured out that took me longer than it should have
- who I was two years ago that I could genuinely help now
- what I could talk about for 30 minutes with no preparation

Don't let me answer in generalities. If I say something vague like
"people interested in fitness," push me to be more specific.

When we're done, give me three versions of this sentence and tell
me which is strongest and why:
"I help [who] who struggle with [what] so they can [outcome]."

Done when you can say your sentence out loud without hedging.

STEP 2

Pick your lane

One topic. Narrower than feels comfortable.

The instinct is to keep it broad so you don't run out of things to say. That instinct is backwards. Broad channels run out faster, because nothing connects to anything else and there's no reason to watch a second video.

Your lane sits where three things overlap: what you already know or are actively learning, what your one person needs, and what you'd still want to talk about in a year.

Prompt

My one person is: [paste your sentence from Step 1].

Help me define a specific content lane for them. Not a broad
category like "business" or "fitness" — something narrow enough
that someone could describe my channel in one sentence.

Then stress-test it:
- Is this narrow enough to be memorable?
- Is it broad enough for 100 videos?
- What would I run out of after 10 videos, and what would keep going?

Give me 3 lane options and tell me which has the longest runway.

Done when you can describe your channel in one sentence and it doesn't need the word "and."

STEP 3

Write down what you'll never do

This takes ten minutes and it saves you from decisions you'd regret later.

Mine, as an example: never say I used something I didn't. Never promise anyone they'll make money. Never recommend based on what pays me. Never fake a result.

You need your own. Write them before you're tempted, not during.

Prompt

I'm starting a content channel about [your lane] for [your person].

Help me write my rules — the things I'll never do, even if they'd
get more views or make more money.

Ask me questions about:
- what makes me distrust other creators in this space
- what I'd be embarrassed to have somebody catch me doing
- what shortcuts exist in this niche that I don't want to take

Then give me the list, short enough that I'd remember it.

Done when you have a short list you could read on camera without flinching.

STEP 4

One job per piece

Every piece of content does one of these five jobs. One. Not two.

  1. Discovery. Gets found by people who don't know you. Short-form, a hook, something surprising.
  2. Authority. Builds trust with somebody who just found you. A real teach, a full walkthrough.
  3. Decision. Helps somebody choose. Comparisons, who it's for, what to skip.
  4. Conversion. Moves somebody to act. The resource, the next step.
  5. Nurture. Keeps the people who already trust you. Email, updates, the behind-the-scenes.

Most beginner content fails because one video tries to do all five. It hooks, teaches, compares, sells and asks for a subscribe, and does none of them well.

Pick the job before you write. Then cut anything that isn't doing it.

Prompt

My lane is [your lane]. My person is [your person].

Build me a content family — five pieces on one topic, where each
does exactly ONE of these jobs: Discovery, Authority, Decision,
Conversion, Nurture.

For each one give me:
- the job it's doing
- the title
- what it promises the viewer
- what it should NOT also try to do
- which piece it points to next

Make them connect, so somebody who watches one has an obvious
reason to watch another.

Done when you have five titles and you know which job each one does.

STEP 5

Build a week you can survive

Not the week you want. The week that still happens when you're tired.

Count your real hours first. Sleep, work, the commute, family, everything else. Then look at what's left. Most people have more than they think and less than they hoped.

Then set two numbers. Your normal week, which is what you do when things go fine. And your floor, which is the smallest thing that still counts on your worst week. One short video. One email. Something.

The floor is the important one, and you have to set it now, while you're motivated. A floor you invent during a bad week is always zero.

Prompt

Here's my actual schedule:
- Sleep: [X] hours
- Work and commute: [X] hours
- Family and other commitments: [X] hours
- Days a week I can realistically show up: [X]

Help me design a content week that fits inside what's actually left.

Give me:
1. A normal week — what I do, which day, roughly how long
2. A minimum week — the smallest version that still counts on a
   bad week
3. What I cut FIRST when something has to give
4. What I never cut

Be realistic. Don't design a schedule that assumes everything goes
well.

Done when you have two numbers written down. Normal, and floor.

STEP 6

Use what you have

Now you can think about gear. Not before.

Here's the honest version, and it should save you money.

  1. Camera. Your phone. Genuinely. Upgrade when something specific is limiting you, not before.
  2. Light. A window, during the day. This is the first thing worth buying, and it's the cheapest.
  3. Audio. Your phone mic, held close. If you only ever upgrade one thing, upgrade this. Audio matters more than video.
  4. Editing. Start on a free editor. Move only when free is actively slowing you down.
  5. Thumbnails. A free design tool. Probably forever.

The order that matters is light, then audio, then everything else. A camera upgrade almost never fixes what people think it'll fix.

The test before buying anything: can you name the specific problem it solves, and has that problem actually happened to you? If not, you're shopping instead of building.

When you get to the editor decision, I walk through three of them, and who each one is for, on the editing page. The microphone question is on the audio page. What I actually use is on What I use. Those pages do include affiliate links, and they're labeled there.

Prompt

I'm making [type of content] for [your person]. I currently have:
[list what you actually own]. My budget is [$X].

Tell me honestly:
1. What can I do right now spending nothing?
2. If I spend nothing, what's the actual limitation — and does it
   matter for the content I'm making?
3. If I spend my budget, what ONE thing should it be, and why?
4. What am I likely to want to buy that I don't need yet?

Don't recommend things I don't need. If the answer is "spend
nothing and start," say that.

Done when you know what you're filming your first one on. Which is probably your phone.

STEP 7

Publish before you're ready

You won't feel ready. That feeling doesn't go away by preparing more. It goes away by publishing.

Your first one will not be good. Mine wasn't. That's not a risk, that's the process. Nobody who finds you at video 30 goes back and picks apart video 1, and if they do, they'll see how far you came.

Three things that make a first piece work:

  1. Open with the most interesting moment, not the first thing that happened. Tension first, context after.
  2. Deliver one useful thing. Somebody who never comes back should still leave with something.
  3. Say what you don't know. It's the fastest trust you'll ever build, and almost nobody does it.

Prompt

I'm making my first piece of content.

Topic: [topic]
Job it's doing: [Discovery / Authority / Decision / Conversion /
Nurture]
My person: [your person]

Write me a simple outline with:
- a cold open using the most interesting moment, not the first one
- one genuinely useful thing they get even if they never come back
- a place where I admit what I don't know yet
- a specific next step at the end

Keep it short enough to make this week. Don't make it clever.
Make it clear.

Done when it's published. Not perfect. Published.

The short version

  1. Name one person.
  2. Pick one lane.
  3. Write your rules.
  4. One job per piece.
  5. Build a week with a floor.
  6. Use what you have.
  7. Publish before you're ready.

That's the whole thing.

What comes after

Once you've published a few times the questions change. You stop asking what to make and start asking why that one worked. That's a better problem, and it's where the real work starts.

I'm doing all of it in public: one episode at a time on YouTube, including the parts that don't work. If you'd rather start from where you are, the three paths are on Start here.
